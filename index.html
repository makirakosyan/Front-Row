Who I am and why

I'm Mariam. I grew up between Sydney & LA, raised by a pink iPod nano blasting  'Ring Ding Dong' and 'Like a G6' in equal measure. 

With a lifetime of attending concerts, festivals, fan activations, and industry-adjacent events across the U.S, Australia, Asia, and beyond, I have been paying close attention to how entertainment companies and agencies build fan ecosystems, expand artists' reach into new markets, and create experiences that actually resonate with local audiences.

With groups like Katseye breaking the mold and reaching heights at record speeds, it feels like watching an entirely new kind of global artist being built in real time.

I believe this is only the beginning, and I want to be a part of what happens next.

Mariam Kirakosyan - Los Angeles

Working with Korean management: structure and culture From what I have observed, Korean entertainment companies appear to operate through a centralised management structure. Embedded in this is a deep respect hierarchy rooted in Korean culture. Seniority, both in age and professional standing, shapes how communication flows inside these organisations. Decisions tend to move upward before they move outward, and direct challenge or informal escalation can be read as disrespectful rather than efficient. The most effective working relationships are built on patience, consistency, and demonstrated respect for the chain of authority. Understanding this is not a minor cultural footnote. It is foundational to working effectively in this space.

03 — Firsthand observation

My experiences with
global touring and live events

Things I have noticed, from the floor up.

i.

High-density vs low-density markets

When ATEEZ returned to Australia in 2026, they upgraded venues from their previous tour and added second shows in both cities after demand outpaced supply. Merchandise sold quickly. Yet for an entire country, there were still only two stops.

The contrast becomes interesting when compared to the US, where a tour may stretch across fifteen cities, each with different audience behaviour, or parts of Asia where fan infrastructure is already deeply established. It highlighted how differently touring appears to operate across high-density and lower-density markets like Australia, particularly when demand seems concentrated but strong.

ii.

Fan culture is portable, but not always one-size-fits-all

At a JustB show in Los Angeles, Korean staff moved through the crowd filming while photostrips were handed out to fans before doors opened. It felt distinctly K-pop, even though we were in LA.

There appears to be a balance between preserving the distinctiveness of K-pop fan culture and adapting experiences in ways that resonate within a different market context. Part of the appeal seems to be that audiences want to participate in something culturally distinct, but localising elements of the experience may also strengthen its impact.

iii.

Bridging the east and west

At ComplexCon Hong Kong, an international crowd queuing for limited streetwear drops were front row for both Yeat and Jennie, with equal energy. The coherence of this moment is increasingly being recognised and tapped into, with artists like Anderson .Paak and 24K Gold collaborating more frequently with Korean acts, as the industry begins to respond to a converging global audience.

Korean artists are not so much crossing into Western street culture as they are occupying it alongside everyone else. CORTIS performing at the NBA All-Star Game reads in a similar way — not as novelty, but as deliberate placement within a space that already carries cultural weight and credibility for American audiences.

There appears to be a growing overlap between spaces once treated as distinct, with audiences already moving fluidly between them. To me, it feels less like a bridge being built and more like one that already exists, with the broader industry now finding ways to meet that shift.

04 — Observational case study

Katseye in Australia:
touching down and generating impact

In this section
  • The setup
  • The venue
  • The format
  • What followed
Context

The setup

Katseye had never performed in Australia and yet by the time they landed in Sydney for a fan Q&A at Luna Park in May 2026, "Gabriela", "Touch" and "Gnarly" were all certified platinum here, with "Debut" and "Gameboy" certified gold. Three of their singles had gone platinum in a market they had never once set foot in.

LOCATION CHOICE

The venue

Luna Park is one of Sydney's most recognisable landmarks, with genuine cultural nostalgia attached to it for anyone who grew up here. Placing a Grammy-nominated group there, rather than a standard promotional venue, meant the setting did contextualising work that builds interest and engagement that even large amounts of marketing spend cannot replicate. This choice gave Australian fans a point of entry that already felt familiar.

Event design

The format

The event was not open to the public. Universal Music Australia ran a competition tied to pre-ordering the new EP Wild, with one grand prize including flights and accommodation from anywhere in Australia. Fans came anyway, some from Wollongong, some from Brisbane, arriving hours early just to be near the venue without tickets.

The Q&A format kept it intimate, a conversation rather than a performance, which I think was well suited to an Australian audience. There is something inherently more down to earth about sitting with an artist and talking than watching them from a distance, and that register tends to land well here. Content creators and local influencers were also in attendance, generating organic coverage across TikTok and Instagram that extended the reach of the event well beyond the room itself.

Momentum

What followed

A surprise free performance was announced for Melbourne's Festival Hall two days later, presented in collaboration with Visit Victoria, American Express, Live Nation, HYBE and Geffen. The combination of exclusivity and intimacy effectively generated momentum in a growing fanbase. The members told Nova 96.9 they loved Australia and would be back. Given the audience response, that feels less like a pleasantry and more like an inevitability.

What Katseye's first visit to Australia made visible is the same thing that has been quietly building across every section of this site — a new kind of global artist operating in a new kind of global moment. Front Row is where I watch it happen.
